🌼 General Event Details

💀 What is the Viva la Vida Festival?

Hosted by the Mexic-Arte Museum, it is Austin's largest and longest-running Día de los Muertos (Day of the Dead) event. This year marks the 43rd annual Viva la Vida Festival & Parade!

24When is the event?

Saturday, October 24, 2026. The grand procession (parade) kicks off at 12:00 PM and runs until 1:00 PM. The main street festival runs from 12:00 PM to 6:00 PM. If you want to walk with Reilly, meet us at 6th Street & Red River Street at 10:00 AM. See the full Day-of Schedule above! 🧡

📍 Where is the festival located?

Downtown Austin! The parade lines up near 6th & Red River and travels about ¾ mile west on Historic 6th Street and south on Congress Avenue to the main festival grounds at 4th Street and Congress Avenue (near Republic Square Park).

💸 How much does it cost?

Nada! Admission to the festival and participation in the parade is completely free and open to all ages. 🎉

🚌 Pro tip: Don't drive downtown!

Downtown parking fills up fast and 6th Street will be closed for the parade, so taking the bus is the easiest, least stressful way to get there.

  • 🚌CapMetro buses: Many routes stop downtown near Congress Avenue. Plan your trip with routes and times on the CapMetro website or the CapMetro app.
  • 🚕Rideshare or drop-off: Ask to be dropped on 5th or 7th Street, since 6th Street will be blocked off.

🚗 If we are driving, where should we park?

There is no assigned parking for parade participants, and downtown parking fills quickly. Give yourself the 9:00 hour to park or get dropped off. Here are some options:

  • 🅿️ City Garages: Austin Convention Center, City Hall Garage, One Texas Center, Palmer Events Center, and RiverSouth.

🌧️ What happens if it rains?

The parade goes on rain or shine! If the weather turns severe, organizers will decide on any delay or cancellation at the last possible moment. We'll pass along any updates, and you can also check the Mexic-Arte Museum event page.

🗺️ Where do we line up, and where does the parade end?

Reilly lines up at 6th & Red River (Parade Section D), right by the parade check-in booth. The parade lines up on 6th Street from 6th & Red River to the I-35 frontage road, travels west on 6th Street, turns south on Congress Avenue, and ends at the festival at 4th & Congress.

After the parade, we're in the pedestrian group: we keep walking south on Congress to the de-staging area at 3rd & Congress (between 4th and 2nd Street). Please don't sit down or linger there. Regroup with your family, head to the exits on 3rd Street, and stay on the sidewalks for safety. To come back to the festival, use the 4th & Brazos Street entrance.

📋 Are there parade rules?

Yes! Everyone walking with us agrees to the Viva la Vida parade guidelines:

  • 👫Stay with the Reilly group for the entire parade.
  • 🧒Children under 18 must be with an adult at all times.
  • 🚫No alcohol or glass containers.
  • 🙏No disorderly conduct or offensive language or materials.
  • 🔥No real candles or fire.
  • 🐕Dogs and other pets must be leashed or held the whole time.
  • 🍬No throwing candy, toys or anything else to the crowd.
  • ➡️Follow the de-staging instructions from Mexic-Arte Museum at the end of the route.

🏡 How do we get back after the parade?

Parade families are welcome to join the Viva la Vida Festival at 4th & Congress (open until 6 PM), with free face painting, vendors, performers and more! After de-staging, come back in through the 4th & Brazos Street entrance. When you're ready to head out, you can walk, take a cab or pedicab, or ride a CapMetro bus back to your car or home.

✨ ¿Sabías Que? ✨

Did you know? Cool cultural nuggets to share with your kids on the way downtown!

🕯️

This year's theme: La Ofrenda

An ofrenda is an altar families build to welcome loved ones' spirits home, with photos, candles and their favorite foods. In this year's parade, artist-made altar pieces are carried down 6th Street and assembled into one giant altar at the festival!

🌼

Flowers that light the way

Orange marigolds, called cempasúchil, are the signature flower of the holiday. Their bright color and strong scent are said to guide spirits back to their families.

💀

Happy skulls, not spooky ones

Smiling calaveras (decorated skulls) celebrate life instead of fearing death. The elegant skeleton lady, La Catrina, was first drawn over 100 years ago by Mexican artist José Guadalupe Posada.

Papel picado

These colorful cut-paper banners flutter in the breeze, and their delicate paper is a reminder of how precious and fragile life is.

🕊️

Two days of remembering

Día de los Muertos is celebrated November 1 and 2. It blends ancient Indigenous traditions from Mexico with Catholic holidays. Families often share pan de muerto, a sweet bread decorated with "bones" made of dough.

🚗

Don't miss at the festival

Bouncing low-rider cars, live music and dance, hands-on art activities, and the Muertos Mercado with 20+ vendors selling Day of the Dead crafts and gifts.
